# Response R04

**Yes. Compound X is terfenadine itself**, rather than a new analogue: its SMILES matches the [PubChem record](https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/compound/5405). Parent terfenadine blocks hERG/IKr at nanomolar concentrations and has a documented history of QT prolongation and torsades de pointes; it was withdrawn from the US market. [Patch clamp study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3417462/); [FDA review](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/nda/2011/201373Orig1s000SumR.pdf).

The key exposure risk is **accumulation of the parent**. CYP3A4 metabolism produces the active antihistamine fexofenadine, which has much less hERG activity. In a clinical interaction study, ketoconazole caused detectable parent terfenadine to accumulate alongside significant QTc prolongation. [Metabolism study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7587944/); [hERG comparison](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1573545/); [interaction study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8445813/).

I would prioritize:

1. **Confirm the sample and run a concentration–response hERG patch clamp assay** on the parent, with fexofenadine as a comparator. Measure actual assay concentrations; terfenadine’s handling can affect the apparent IC₅₀. [FDA assay guidance](https://www.fda.gov/media/161198/download).
2. **Quantify parent and metabolites** in human hepatocytes, including a CYP3A4 inhibition condition. Combine that with protein binding and projected exposure to assess the hERG margin for *unbound parent* under elevated exposure conditions. [FDA guidance](https://www.fda.gov/media/161198/download).
3. **Redirect lead optimization away from X itself.** For replacement compounds, establish H₁ activity and exposure, then repeat hERG testing and, for viable candidates, assess cardiac electrophysiology and in vivo QT at relevant exposures.

Given terfenadine’s established clinical liability, I would treat X as a **stop signal for this structure**, not wait for a routine screen to decide whether hERG matters.
