## If you want model attribution anyway

The tractable approach is fragment ablation rather than gradient/attention saliency, which is notoriously unstable for transformer chemistry models:

1. Neutralize the basic amine — swap `N(CH3)` for an ether O, an amide N, or a sulfone-adjacent N.
2. Truncate one aryl arm at a time.
3. Replace each methanesulfonamide with H, or with an acetamide.

Re-score each and look at the Δprobability. The catch: every one of those analogs is a different molecule that may fall further outside the domain, so a probability drop can reflect distribution shift rather than the model "using" that group. Treat the result as a hypothesis about model behavior, and interpret cautiously.

## On mechanism — the part the model can't give you

What's independently established about dofetilide and hERG (from mutagenesis, state-dependence, and structural work, not from QSAR):

- Block is **open/inactivated-state-dependent pore block** from the intracellular side; the drug reaches the inner cavity and occludes it.
- The **protonated tertiary amine** and the **aromatic rings** are the canonical interaction features — Tyr652 and Phe656 on the S6 helix are the classic mutagenesis hits, with the amine implicated in cation-π/aromatic contacts and the rings in hydrophobic packing. The amine-plus-two-lipophilic-arms arrangement is the textbook hERG pharmacophore, and dofetilide fits it cleanly.
- The **two methanesulfonamides** are largely the class III / IKr-pharmacology features. They're not what a mechanistic model of pore block hangs on.

So if an ablation study did flag the amine and the aryl arms, that would be a pleasing coincidence with the known pharmacophore — but it would be exactly that. A sequence classifier trained on binary actives/inactives learns correlations between substructures and labels. It has no representation of the channel, the pore, gating states, or a binding pose. It cannot distinguish pore block from trafficking inhibition, and it cannot tell you which residues matter.
