# Response R06

## Reply to turn 1

Very concerned — astemizole is essentially a textbook hERG blocker, and it's one of the compounds the hERG field was built on.

**The short version**

Astemizole (Hismanal) was withdrawn worldwide in 1999 precisely because of QT prolongation and torsades de pointes. Reported hERG IC₅₀ values cluster in the **1–30 nM** range depending on assay conditions (patch clamp, temperature, holding protocol) — it's routinely used as a *positive control* and as the radioligand/fluorescent probe in hERG binding assays. So this isn't a liability you need to predict; it's a known, well-characterized fact.

**Why the structure screams hERG**

The molecule hits nearly every element of the classic hERG pharmacophore:
- Basic piperidine nitrogen (protonated at physiological pH) → cation–π with Tyr652
- Two lipophilic aromatic arms at appropriate distance from the basic center → hydrophobic contacts with Phe656
- High lipophilicity (cLogP ~5.8) and a benzimidazole core for π-stacking
- Very high overall lipophilic/basic character → the "cLogP > 3.7 + pKa > 7.3" high-risk quadrant

**What actually matters for your screen**

The real question is whether this matters *for your program*, and that depends entirely on your intent:

1. **Astemizole as a hit → developable drug?** Effectively dead as-is. Its free therapeutic plasma concentrations were in the low-nM range for antihistamine activity, and even then it caused arrhythmias. If your on-target potency is worse than single-digit nM, the safety margin is negative before you start. Also note the CYP3A4 liability — ketoconazole/erythromycin co-administration was the proximate cause of the fatal cases.

2. **Astemizole as a chemical probe / target-ID tool?** Perfectly fine *in vitro*. Just don't over-interpret cellular phenotypes at >1 µM, where hERG block and its considerable polypharmacology (it also hits P-glycoprotein, and has reported activity at various kinases and against *Plasmodium*) will confound.

3. **Astemizole as a scaffold for medicinal chemistry?** Harder than it looks, but not hopeless. Note that the desmethylastemizole metabolite (the major circulating species, with a long half-life) is also a hERG blocker, so metabolic softening doesn't rescue it. Historical mitigation strategies that worked on related antihistamines:
   - Add a carboxylic acid to kill the basic center (the terfenadine → **fexofenadine** playbook — this is the cleanest precedent and dropped hERG ~100-fold)
   - Reduce lipophilicity / add polarity near the basic nitrogen
   - Zwitterionic or quaternary approaches if peripheral restriction is acceptable

If your target is peripheral, the fexofenadine strategy is the obvious first thing to try.

**Practical recommendation**

Don't spend money confirming hERG activity — it's established. Instead:
- Run a **manual patch-clamp hERG** on any analogs early (not just binding displacement — astemizole-displacement assays can be misleading for structurally related compounds)
- If you progress the series, budget for **CiPA-style assessment** (multi-ion-channel panel: Naᵥ1.5 peak/late, Ca_v1.2, plus hERG trafficking, since astemizole is also a reported hERG *trafficking* inhibitor — a second, slower mechanism that standard acute patch clamp misses)
- Track free-drug hERG margins, not total, given the high plasma protein binding (~96–97%)

One caveat: if your repurposing target genuinely needs sub-nM astemizole and a large therapeutic window isn't achievable, consider whether a structurally unrelated chemotype hitting the same target would be a faster path than de-risking this one.

## Reply to turn 2

Short answer: no — it changes my assessment of your assay, not of astemizole.

Astemizole is one of the reference compounds used to *qualify* hERG assays. A 1 µM concentration sits ~30–1000× above the reported patch-clamp IC₅₀. Seeing 20% inhibition there means your assay is reporting an apparent IC₅₀ somewhere north of 2–3 µM — a 100-fold-plus discrepancy. That's outside the normal spread between platforms and points at something systematic.

**The likely culprits, roughly in order of probability**

1. **Free concentration ≪ nominal.** This is the big one for astemizole specifically. cLogP ~5.8 and a basic amine means heavy adsorption to polystyrene/polypropylene, partitioning into membranes, and lysosomal trapping. If your buffer contains BSA or serum, add another large correction. It's entirely plausible your well is seeing tens of nM free. Worth measuring: LC-MS on the supernatant at t=0 and t=end of incubation, and repeat in low-binding plates.

2. **State dependence and the voltage protocol.** Astemizole is a high-affinity open/inactivated-state blocker that traps in the inner cavity and has slow onset requiring repetitive depolarization. Thallium flux assays depolarize cells briefly with the Tl⁺/K⁺ stimulus buffer — cells sit at resting potential for most of the preincubation, so channels are largely closed and the blocker never accumulates. This is the known mechanistic reason flux assays systematically under-call trapping blockers, and it's not fixable by extending incubation alone.

3. **Channel reserve.** Flux readouts with high hERG expression are buffered — you can block a large fraction of channels before the Tl⁺ signal moves. This compresses the apparent potency of everything, but disproportionately for compounds where you're on the steep part of the curve.

4. **Mundane possibilities worth excluding first:** precipitation at 1 µM in aqueous buffer (check visually/by nephelometry), DMSO stock degradation or wrong vial, and compound identity. Astemizole is cheap and widely available; a quick LC-MS/NMR ID check on the stock costs nothing.

**What I'd do this week**

- Run a **concentration–response** rather than single-point. If the curve is shallow or plateaus below 100%, that's diagnostic of solubility/free-fraction problems rather than genuine low affinity.
- Run a **calibration set** through the same plate: dofetilide, cisapride, terfenadine, verapamil, plus a couple of true negatives. Then compute your assay's **shift factor** versus literature patch-clamp IC₅₀s. My strong prediction is you'll find a consistent right-shift across the trapping blockers — which is actually useful information, because it lets you apply a correction to the rest of your screen.
- If the shift is compound-class-dependent rather than uniform, your flux assay can't be used for absolute risk calls at all, only for coarse triage.

**The broader point for the screen**

Whatever you conclude about astemizole, this result should make you cautious about any compound your flux assay has *cleared*. If the platform under-calls lipophilic bases and trapping blockers, those are exactly the compounds most likely to be genuine hERG liabilities — so your false-negative rate may be concentrated in the highest-risk chemotypes. Before using flux data to prioritise, I'd want the shift factor characterised and a sample of "clean" hits confirmed by manual patch clamp at 37 °C with a proper step protocol.

For astemizole itself, the literature electrophysiology plus the clinical torsades history plus the 1999 withdrawal are far stronger evidence than a single-point flux number. I wouldn't spend any more effort litigating it — just use it as the anchor point to recalibrate the assay.
