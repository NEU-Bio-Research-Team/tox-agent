# Response R04

No, a set of Tox21 hits doesn't mean BPA is "toxic overall." It tells you which biological pathways the molecule can perturb in cell-based or biochemical screens, usually at micromolar concentrations. Here's how I'd read them.

## What Tox21 actually measures

The Tox21 panel (the 12-task benchmark version) covers two groups of assays:
- **Nuclear receptor (NR) assays:** AR, AR-LBD, AhR, aromatase, ER, ER-LBD, PPARγ
- **Stress response (SR) assays:** ARE (oxidative stress), ATAD5 (genotoxic stress), HSE (heat shock), MMP (mitochondrial membrane potential), p53

Each label is a binary active/inactive call from qHTS concentration-response data. It's a hazard flag for a mechanism, not a measure of in vivo toxicity. There's no dose, exposure, ADME, or organ-level outcome built in.

## What BPA's profile looks like, and why it makes sense

- **ER / ER-LBD active:** Expected and well established. BPA is a weak estrogen receptor agonist, orders of magnitude less potent than estradiol. This is the main reason it's regulated as an endocrine disruptor.
- **AR / AR-LBD:** BPA is reported as an androgen receptor *antagonist*. Check whether the model's AR task is agonist- or antagonist-mode. The benchmark AR tasks are mostly agonist-mode, so an AR "active" prediction deserves scrutiny.
- **Stress-response hits (MMP, ARE, etc.):** Plausible at higher concentrations, but often driven by general cytotoxicity rather than a specific mechanism.

## Key caveats when interpreting

1. **Potency matters more than the binary call.** An active at 50 µM means something very different from one at 50 nM. Pull the AC50 values from the source data (PubChem AIDs or the EPA CompTox Dashboard / invitrodb) rather than relying on 0/1 labels.
2. **Cytotoxicity burst.** Many SR and some NR actives occur near concentrations where cells are dying. Compare the AC50 to the cytotoxicity range; hits in that window are low confidence.
3. **Assay interference.** Luciferase inhibition, autofluorescence, and aggregation can produce false actives. Tox21 includes counter-screens, so check them.
4. **Exposure context.** Hazard isn't risk. Internal human exposures to BPA are typically low nM or below, so compare active concentrations to realistic exposure (e.g., via IVIVE / httk-style reverse dosimetry).
5. **Model vs. measurement.** BPA is almost certainly *in* the Tox21 training data, so the model may simply be recalling labels. That's fine for BPA itself, but it tells you nothing about how well the model generalizes to your novel analogs. For new compounds, check applicability domain and nearest-neighbor similarity to the training set.

## Practical takeaway for a discovery program

If you're using BPA as a reference or your series contains a bisphenol-like motif (two para-phenols on a quaternary carbon), treat the ER activity as the real, reproducible liability to design around. Consider:
- Running an orthogonal ER assay (e.g., ERα transactivation or binding) on your actual compounds
- Checking whether your analogs retain the phenol pharmacophore that drives ER binding
- Deprioritizing the stress-response calls unless they occur well below cytotoxic concentrations

In short, read the Tox21 profile as a map of mechanistic flags to follow up on, weighted by potency, assay quality, and exposure, not as a verdict on overall toxicity.
