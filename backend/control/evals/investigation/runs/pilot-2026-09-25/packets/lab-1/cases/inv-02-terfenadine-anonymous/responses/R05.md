# Response R05

**Yes—hERG is a major concern.** The supplied SMILES matches **terfenadine**, a former allergic-rhinitis drug, exactly. [PubChem](https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/compound/5405) Terfenadine blocks hERG in measured patch-clamp experiments, and accumulation of the parent drug—particularly when its metabolism was inhibited—was linked to QT prolongation and serious arrhythmias. It was withdrawn in the US after the safer active metabolite, fexofenadine, became available. [Patch-clamp study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3417462/) · [FDA history](https://www.fda.gov/about-fda/fda-history-exhibits/drug-therapeutics-regulation-us)

ToxPred’s **0.68 blocker probability** exceeds its **0.413 threshold**, but it is a classification score, not an IC₅₀ or a clinical risk estimate. Its applicability check only confirms familiar elements; it does not establish structural similarity to the training set. Here, the exact compound identity and experimental history carry more weight than the model output.

**I would deprioritize X as a lead.** If you need internal data to document the decision or guide an analogue series, the next tests are:

1. Measure a hERG concentration–response by patch clamp for X and promising analogues, with verified assay concentrations.
2. Measure H1 potency, parent-drug metabolism, plasma protein binding, and predicted **unbound parent exposure** at an effective dose. Check how CYP3A4 inhibition changes parent exposure; that interaction was central to terfenadine’s clinical problem. [Clinical interaction study](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/articlepdf/404621/jama_269_12_025.pdf)
3. For an analogue that still merits advancement, compare hERG potency with anticipated high unbound exposure, then assess cardiac ion channels and in vivo QT effects as appropriate. [ICH E14/S7B guidance](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/e14-and-s7b-clinical-and-nonclinical-evaluation-qtqtc-interval-prolongation-and-proarrhythmic)

The three positive Tox21 calls are secondary screening signals; they do not change the immediate cardiac-safety decision.
