# Response R03

**Short answer: no — fexofenadine is essentially the textbook case of a successful hERG de-risking, and the margin at therapeutic exposure is large.**

**Why the question is reasonable to ask.** The molecule contains a canonical hERG pharmacophore: a basic piperidine nitrogen (pKa ~9) flanked by a diphenylmethanol head group and a lipophilic aryl tail — exactly the arrangement that gives high-affinity block via Tyr652/Phe656 in the inner cavity. Its parent, terfenadine, is one of the most potent hERG blockers ever marketed (IC50 ~10–60 nM) and was withdrawn in the late 1990s after QT prolongation/torsades, especially with CYP3A4 inhibitors (ketoconazole, erythromycin, grapefruit).

**What changed.** Fexofenadine is terfenadine's *tert*-butyl oxidation metabolite — the carboxylic acid. That single change does two things:
- Creates a zwitterion, killing the net cationic character needed for productive interaction with the hERG inner cavity. Reported hERG IC50s fall in the tens of micromolar (order ~10–100 µM depending on assay/temperature), i.e. a ~1000-fold potency drop versus terfenadine.
- Drops logD sharply and makes the compound a P-gp/OATP substrate with low passive permeability — low CNS exposure (hence non-sedating) and no reliance on CYP3A4 for clearance, so the DDI-driven exposure spikes that killed terfenadine don't occur (fexofenadine is largely excreted unchanged; transporter-mediated DDIs shift AUC only ~2-fold).

**Exposure math.** Cmax at standard doses is roughly 200–700 ng/mL ≈ 0.4–1.4 µM total, with ~60–70% plasma protein binding → free Cmax on the order of 0.15–0.5 µM. Against a hERG IC50 in the tens of µM, that's a free margin of ~50–200x, comfortably above the ≥30x total / ~100x screening thresholds most groups use. Clinical QT studies, including supratherapeutic dosing well above label and co-administration with ketoconazole/erythromycin, showed no meaningful QTc effect.

**Practical read-across for your program.** Two things worth taking from this:
1. Appending an ionizable acid (or a zwitterion, or shifting amine pKa down) remains one of the highest-yield hERG mitigations, and it often buys CNS exclusion for free. The trade-off you'll pay is permeability and oral bioavailability — fexofenadine's F is only ~30% and it has high inter-individual variability from transporter involvement.
2. Don't judge the parent by the metabolite or vice versa. If you're carrying an analog with a *tert*-butyl-on-aryl motif, check whether the acid metabolite is the circulating species — and equally, whether a low-hERG parent can generate a *more* cationic/lipophilic metabolite that reintroduces liability. Metabolite hERG profiling is the step most often skipped.

If you're benchmarking a series against this scaffold, I'd suggest measuring hERG at 10 and 30 µM patch-clamp rather than relying on a binding displacement assay — the zwitterionic compounds in this chemotype tend to give misleadingly flat binding data.
