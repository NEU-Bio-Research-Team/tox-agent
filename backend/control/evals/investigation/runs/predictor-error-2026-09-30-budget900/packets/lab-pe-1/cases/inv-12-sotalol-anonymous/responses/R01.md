# Response R01

## Short answer: don't trust the "non_blocker" call here — this compound is sotalol.

`CC(C)NCC(O)c1ccc(NS(C)(=O)=O)cc1` is N-[4-[1-hydroxy-2-(isopropylamino)ethyl]phenyl]methanesulfonamide, i.e. **sotalol**, a marketed class III antiarrhythmic whose *mechanism of action* is IKr (hERG) blockade, and which carries a boxed warning for QT prolongation and torsades de pointes. It is one of the CiPA reference high-TdP-risk compounds and is routinely used as a positive control in QT work.

## Why ToxPred says non-blocker, and why it isn't strictly wrong

This is a classic potency-vs-risk mismatch rather than a random miss:

- Sotalol's hERG IC50 is genuinely weak — roughly 100–500 µM in patch clamp, depending on protocol. Most hERG classification models are trained against a potency cut (commonly 10 µM), so on that definition sotalol sits on the "non-blocker" side. p = 0.31 vs a 0.41 threshold is a soft, low-confidence call sitting in exactly that grey zone.
- What makes it torsadogenic is the **dosing**, not the potency: therapeutic plasma levels are in the tens of µM with low protein binding, so the free-concentration-to-IC50 margin collapses to single digits. A binary potency label cannot see that, because it has no exposure term.

Take-home for how you read this output: a "non_blocker" label from this model means "probably not potent," not "no proarrhythmic liability." The Tox21 panel is all clean too, which is unsurprising and unrelated — none of those 12 assays interrogate cardiac ion channels.

## Structural alert you should flag manually

The **aryl methanesulfonamide + β-aminoethanol** motif is the canonical class III antiarrhythmic pharmacophore: dofetilide, ibutilide, E-4031, almokalant and sotalol all share the methanesulfonanilide. If other members of this screening set carry that head group, treat them as hERG-suspect regardless of what the model returns.

## What I'd do next

1. **Check provenance before any chemistry.** A marketed drug appearing as a "screening hit" usually means either (a) the plate contains an annotated drug-repurposing library, or (b) there's a registration/deduplication problem. Run the set through a known-drug lookup (ChEMBL/DrugBank/InChIKey match) and see how many other approved agents come back. That's a 30-minute job and may reframe the whole hit list.
2. **Don't run a de novo hERG assay to "confirm" this.** The literature is deep — manual patch clamp, automated platforms, CiPA in-silico models, and clinical TQT data all exist. Pull the numbers instead.
3. **If sotalol's target activity is the hit** (i.e. it's active on your primary assay for an unrelated reason), decide whether that's mechanism or a class-III artifact, then look for chemically unrelated confirmatory hits. Building an SAR series off this scaffold would be walking into IKr blockade deliberately.
4. **Calibrate the model, not just this compound.** Sotalol is a useful sentinel: add it plus dofetilide, cisapride, terfenadine, verapamil and a few CiPA low-risk drugs as a standing control set for ToxPred. That will tell you empirically where the 0.41 threshold sits relative to clinically relevant risk, and whether you need a second gate.
5. **Consider an exposure-aware layer.** Since the failure mode here is missing margin rather than missing binding, a downstream check that combines predicted/measured hERG IC50 with projected free Cmax (or a simple safety-margin flag) would catch this class of compound. Purely structural classifiers won't.

One caveat on the applicability domain block: it only confirms the elements are common, and explicitly says it cannot establish distributional similarity. Treat it as no evidence of coverage rather than positive evidence — it does not add confidence to the negative call.
